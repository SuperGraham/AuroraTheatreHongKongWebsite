drama teachers challenges and how to overcome them

Aurora Theatre — 9 Apr 2018

Every teacher faces challenges, inside and outside the classroom. When teaching drama, the challenges can become even more intense, depending on the location and the nationalities of the students. In Hong Kong, we deal with pupils who not only struggle to keep up with the English, but are immensely pressured by tests, exams, and academic results. Very often, drama lessons are mistaken for “playground time” and schools and parents tend to prioritize other activities without realizing that there is so much more to drama than having a break from academia.

As we happen to deal with actors who are also drama coaches (one has to earn a living somehow and Hong Kong is a pretty expensive city), we have asked around to understand better what are some of the main challenges faced by coaches and instructors and how, eventually, you may be able to overcome them.

Problem 1:

Poor English Level.

This happens in any country where English is not the first language, and yet students have to master it just as well as their first. Some students can receive an early foreign education, but the majority cannot as not everybody can afford it. Things become even more difficult when the class is populated by students from mainland China who need to master Cantonese first before they tackle English.

Bottomline: everything becomes incredibly frustrating for the teacher in charge, who speaks, explains and tries hard and yet no one can understand what he/she says.

Solution:

Don’t get frustrated. At the end of the day, it’s not their fault. Not everyone is lucky enough to go to tutorial English classes or have a private tutor. Keep your energy, and your smile, going.

Musical games and activities, in this particular case,  become your best friends. If you are also planning to direct the group in a mini-school performance, add as many songs and musical numbers as you can. There will always be that student who doesn’t like singing/dancing, but once the majority is into it, even the most unwilling pupil will follow.

Problem 2:

Lack of creativity.

In Asia, the academic approach to education is everything. Hong Kong, of course, is no exception. Students begin classes and activities before they start walking or speaking. After all, the competition becomes tougher and tougher every year and parents need to make sure that their children will enter good schools. This whole approach, however, creates unhealthy dynamics for students who get pressured more and more every day into learning academic subjects only to score high marks. As a consequence, students are not used to thinking in a creative/critical way as they don’t receive enough tools to do so, and when students finally approach the drama class, they are really limited in making choices (read more about the importance of learning drama here).

Solution:

The drama classroom is the best platform to learn how to think outside the box, but it has to happen gradually.

Guide students step-by-step. If your students are in primary classes, when playing games and activities, ask them a lot of questions:

e.g.: If you are a particular animal, how do you move? What do you eat? How do you sound like? What makes you angry? What makes you scared?

If you are approaching a script with your students, break down the story to them in an easy way to understand, and focus on the surroundings and the characters.

e.g.: What is your character doing? What does he want? What gets in the way of getting it? What kind of surroundings does your character find him/herself in? What is happening to your character? What did she/he do before that scene?

The more questions you ask, the more students are forced to think about it. Avoid lecturing them about the play/characters/story you are approaching together, as students tend to be passive and quiet already. Lecturing them on something will make them even quieter.

Problem 3:

The “reputation” of drama.

For many, drama is not a subject, but playtime where kids can run around and shout and make as much noise as they want and the teacher is just there to make sure no one gets hurt. After all, students are somewhat oppressed and need to express themselves somehow.

Wrong.

Drama is very much a subject. Hong Kong has luckily raised the bar when it became an elective in DSE (and rather demanding, too, considering that recently the plays analyzed over the exams were Othello and The Crucible), but we still need to do a lot of work to rectify the common misconception of what drama really is.

For one thing, drama involves a great deal of discipline, and while teachers need to keep things fun and lighthearted in order to keep drama popular, they also need to keep the class in place as usually there are not too many sessions available, and productivity must always be efficient.

Solution:

Focus on the students, and make sure they understand what drama is all about. If they understand it, everything else will follow. Once again, small steps, but steady and consistent.

First of all, make sure to have a structured class:

Begin with a warm-up game

Introduce activities that are targeted toward the script/play you are working on

Put students into groups, and let them rehearse their scenes

Have a short break

Revise all the work that has been done so far, and give feedback and encouragement. Always start from what works (which puts everyone in a good mood – including yourself) and correct what is needed without imposing.

A break is not always possible since time might be limited, but in case you are coaching a two-hour session then a break is a must, as no one can keep such focus for that long (and probably, nor can you).

Before schools and parents sign up for classes, make sure to present them with a breakdown of what will happen, so they’ll know what to expect. Those who teach at learning centres must make sure to be particularly well organized, and have a vast range of activities, especially when dealing with small groups (Find a great activity resource here). And the latter brings us to our next issue:

Problem 4:

Students not keen on drama.

Fair enough. Not everyone is cut out for the performing arts, in the same way not everyone is cut out for sports, academia, and so forth.

But what happens when we deal with children in the classroom who don’t really want to be there and yet the parents, or the school, insist that the students be there regardless of whether they want to be part of it or not?

In every drama class, students should always have something to do, which keeps them productive and prevents them from fidgeting and making noise. But what to do when students sit there passively staring at the dead air, giving you nothing to work with at all?

Solution:

Involve them backstage.

Drama is not just about acting and performing, it’s also about learning to work together. If you give other tasks to students unwilling to act, you will see them react for being understood, and chances are they’ll prove to themselves, and to you, that they can do other things if they have the motivation to do so.

Understand their personality: are they good at drawing, or at crafts making? Are they good at playing the piano, or any musical instrument for that matter? Create groups for set design, props making, costume sourcing, music, lighting, and backstage work. Let students choose the area of choice, and let them work together. Make sure they keep you up-to-date with their choices, and that they use their time wisely. Set deadlines where each time they have to show you the progress of their work.

Putting students into categories also works for those students with smaller roles who, once they are off stage, don’t have much to do and need to do something with their time.

Problem 5:

Students criticizing the teachers’ activities.

This is a very common problem, and it’s quite frustrating when it happens. Some students think it’s ok to challenge the teacher by commenting on certain drama activities by saying that they are either silly or boring, refusing to participate.

Solution:

Set rules before beginning your lessons where certain behaviors are allowed and others are not (such as mentioning the B word) and explain constructive criticism to your students.

Make them understand that labeling something as “boring” is not productive nor useful to anyone, as there is no constructive feedback, and once they understand it, encourage them to comment on what they don’t like, but in a more productive way.

Very often they realize that they have talked without really realizing what the activity is about. This class behaviour will make them think before they speak. Local students don’t really want to put up with long arguments and fights; as long as they understand early that they must comment with reasons rather than for the sake of it, they will drop the attitude quicker and be more attentive.

Make sure the activities are suitable for their age group: children do well in musical/active games, whereas teenagers are more self-conscious in acting over the top in front of their peers. Choose activities that will help them drop the barrier in a gradual way.

In conclusion, focusing on the group you have and understanding their personalities and language abilities will help you play around it. There must always be a balance between play and work, and there must be clarity between you and your student. Structure, be prepared, be ready, and those challenges can really turn in your favour.

What other challenges have you faced? Is there anything else you can think of? Feel free to share any thought with us, as we always want to hear from you all.

Have a wonderful start to the week!

Aurora xx
